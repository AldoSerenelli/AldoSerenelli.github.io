Standard solar models

Standard solar models (SSMs) constitute a reference framework for several areas of research, including solar and stellar structure, solar neutrino physics, and particle physics. Their underlying philosophy is to provide a well-defined and internally consistent description of the Sun, enabling quantitative predictions with a minimal number of free parameters and calibrations. SSMs, like all theoretical models, represent an idealized and incomplete description of the Sun.

By construction, the evolution of SSMs over time is primarily driven by updates to their physical inputs, such as the solar chemical composition, nuclear reaction cross sections, radiative opacities, and the equation of state. The inclusion of additional physical processes—such as rotation or magnetic fields—is conventionally classified as belonging to non-standard solar models. Although this distinction is to some extent arbitrary, processes excluded from SSMs generally require additional assumptions or tunable parameters, which tend to reduce the predictive power of non-SSMs.

Despite their limitations, SSMs have provided a successful framework for interpreting helioseismic and neutrino observations and remain a benchmark for stellar modeling.

Complementary views of the interior

Helioseismology and solar neutrinos constrain different aspects of solar structure and its physical inputs. Comparing these observations with model predictions helps expose degeneracies between composition, nuclear reaction rates and radiative opacity.

The B23 generation incorporates the nuclear reaction inputs used in Solar Fusion III and provides models for several solar compositions, including MB22 and AAG21. The model files and supporting tools are organized in a dedicated data collection.

Nuclear astrophysics and solar modelling

Nuclear reaction rates are essential inputs to solar models: they govern energy generation and the production of solar neutrinos. Connecting nuclear experiments and theory with solar modelling allows us to assess how uncertainties in these rates affect predictions of the solar interior and its observable properties.

My work in this area includes Solar Fusion III, a community evaluation of the nuclear reactions that power hydrogen-burning stars, and LUNANOVA, the ERC Synergy project that brings precision nuclear measurements together with solar and stellar modelling. Within LUNANOVA, these connections are central to improving model predictions and interpreting increasingly precise observations of the Sun.

Selected Work

Solar fusion III: New data and theory for hydrogen-burning stars — Acharya et al. (2025)

Reviews experimental and theoretical advances in the nuclear reactions that power hydrogen-burning stars, recommending reaction inputs and their uncertainties. The review also examines solar neutrino measurements, radiative opacities and electron screening, providing an updated foundation for solar and stellar models.

DOIarXiv

Status of direct determination of solar neutrino fluxes after Borexino — Gonzalez-Garcia et al. (2024)

Determines solar neutrino fluxes through a global analysis of solar and terrestrial neutrino data, including the final results from all three phases of Borexino. Comparisons with standard solar models assess how conclusions depend on the luminosity constraint and assumptions about CNO neutrino fluxes.

DOIarXiv

Observational constraints on the origin of the elements. IV. Standard composition of the Sun — Magg et al. (2022)

Reassesses the solar chemical composition using high-quality spectra, updated atomic data and non-LTE modelling with several atmospheric models. Solar models computed with the revised abundances show improved agreement with helioseismic constraints on the solar interior.
